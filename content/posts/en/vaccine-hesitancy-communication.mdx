Public discussion of vaccine hesitancy often collapses into two camps: people who "trust science" and people who don't. The communication and public health research on this topic supports a far less binary picture — one in which hesitancy is a spectrum of reasons, shaped by trust, access, and lived experience, that rarely responds well to a single message delivered louder. This is an **evidence overview** of how researchers in this field actually frame the problem.

## The "3 Cs" framing

A widely used framework in public health, developed by a working group convened by the World Health Organization, describes vaccine hesitancy through three broad drivers: **confidence** (trust in vaccine safety, effectiveness, and the systems that deliver them), **complacency** (perceived low risk of the disease itself), and **convenience** (practical access — cost, distance, time off work, appointment availability). The framework has since been extended by some researchers with additional dimensions, but the core insight has held up well: hesitancy driven by low confidence looks very different from hesitancy driven by poor convenience, and needs a different response.

This matters because a campaign built entirely around "the vaccine is safe and effective" messaging will do little for someone whose real barrier is that the nearest clinic is an hour away and only open during their work shift — and vice versa, expanding clinic hours will do little for someone with a specific, unaddressed safety concern.

## Why "just give people the facts" often underperforms

A recurring finding in health communication research is that factual correction alone is a weaker intervention than intuition suggests, and can occasionally backfire with audiences who already distrust the source delivering the correction. This isn't an argument against accuracy — accuracy is necessary — but it is an argument that the messenger, the format, and the relationship of trust matter as much as the content of the message itself.

Research on trusted messengers consistently finds that people are more receptive to health information from sources they already have a relationship with — a family doctor, a community or religious leader, a pharmacist — than from unfamiliar institutional messaging, even when the underlying content is identical. This is one of the more replicated and practically useful findings in the vaccine communication literature.

## Listening as a communication strategy

Public health communicators increasingly emphasise structured listening — understanding the specific, often locally-varying reasons behind hesitancy in a given community — before designing a messaging campaign. Reasons that look similar on the surface ("I'm not sure about the vaccine") can stem from very different underlying concerns: historical mistreatment by medical institutions, specific safety questions, low perceived personal risk, or simple logistical friction. Effective interventions tend to be the ones that correctly diagnose which of these is actually operating before responding to it.

> "Hesitancy" is not the opposite of "acceptance" — it's a spectrum, and most people who express hesitancy still go on to vaccinate. Treating the entire spectrum as a single, uniform resistance to overcome tends to produce blunt, ineffective messaging.

### Key takeaways

- Vaccine hesitancy is commonly analysed through three drivers — confidence, complacency, and convenience — which call for different responses.
- Purely factual correction is a weaker standalone intervention than commonly assumed, particularly across low-trust relationships.
- Trusted messengers (existing relationships) tend to outperform unfamiliar institutional sources delivering the same content.
- Effective communication strategies generally start with understanding the specific, local reasons behind hesitancy rather than assuming a single cause.

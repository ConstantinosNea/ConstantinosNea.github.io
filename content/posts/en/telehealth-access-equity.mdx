The rapid expansion of telehealth was, for a while, treated almost as settled progress: care would follow the patient instead of the other way around, and geography, mobility, and scheduling constraints would matter less. This piece is an **analysis** — my reading of what the evidence and reporting since that expansion suggest about who actually gained better access, and who was left navigating a new set of barriers instead of the old ones.

## The promise, briefly

The case for telehealth as an equity tool was intuitive: it removes travel time and cost, reduces the need to take time off work, helps people in areas with few local specialists, and can lower the friction of seeking care for stigmatised conditions, including many mental health concerns. For a meaningful number of patients, that promise has held up — particularly for routine follow-ups, medication management, and some mental health services.

## Where the digital divide reasserts itself

The complication is that telehealth doesn't remove access barriers so much as it trades one set for another. Video-based care assumes reliable broadband, a private space to have a sensitive conversation, a suitable device, and a working level of digital literacy. Each of those assumptions fails disproportionately for some of the same populations telehealth was meant to help: older adults, lower-income households, rural communities with limited broadband infrastructure, and people with limited English proficiency or disabilities that make video interfaces harder to use.

Audio-only telehealth has emerged as a partial workaround in some settings, and evidence suggests it materially expands access for people without reliable video capability — but it is not always reimbursed on the same terms as video visits, which creates a policy-driven access gap layered on top of an infrastructure one.

## What the shift actually changed

A fair reading of the post-expansion evidence is not "telehealth failed" or "telehealth succeeded" — it's that telehealth functions as an additional access channel rather than a replacement for in-person care, and its equity impact depends heavily on implementation details: reimbursement parity between audio and video visits, broadband investment, device lending programmes, and interpreter availability in virtual settings. Where those details were handled well, access gaps narrowed. Where they weren't, telehealth risked widening them by concentrating the benefit among patients who were already comparatively well-connected.

## The policy angle that gets less attention

Much of the public conversation about telehealth focuses on the technology. The more consequential variable, in my reading of the analysis and reporting on this shift, is reimbursement policy — whether insurers and public payers treat telehealth visits (audio and video) as equivalent to in-person visits for payment purposes. Coverage rules, not technical capability, are frequently the binding constraint on whether a clinic can sustainably offer telehealth to the patients who need it most.

### Key takeaways

- Telehealth genuinely improved access for many patients, especially for follow-up care and some mental health services.
- Video-based care assumes broadband, privacy, and digital literacy that are unevenly distributed — often along the same lines as pre-existing access gaps.
- Audio-only telehealth expands access for people without reliable video, but reimbursement rules don't always treat it equally to video visits.
- Reimbursement and infrastructure policy, more than the technology itself, largely determine whether telehealth narrows or widens equity gaps.

> **This article is commentary.** It combines the author's personal opinion with general, well-established public health evidence on heat and health. Opinion and evidence are distinguished throughout.

Every summer, the same pattern repeats: a heatwave arrives, hospitals see a rise in heat-related admissions, and within a week or two, attention moves on. I find this genuinely strange, and — this is my opinion — I think it reflects a mismatch between how dangerous extreme heat actually is and how *dramatic* it looks compared to other hazards that receive faster, more visible emergency responses.

## A hazard without a single dramatic moment

Public health and climate research consistently identify extreme heat as one of the more dangerous weather-related hazards, in part because heat-related deaths are frequently under-recorded — heat often acts as a contributing factor to cardiovascular or respiratory death rather than being listed as the primary cause, which makes the true toll harder to see in standard mortality statistics. Unlike a storm or flood, a heatwave doesn't damage buildings or make for dramatic footage; it just quietly raises the risk of death for people with existing health vulnerabilities, particularly older adults, outdoor workers, people without reliable access to cooling, and people with chronic cardiovascular or respiratory conditions.

## What the evidence actually establishes

Separating opinion from evidence here: climate science bodies have documented an increase in the frequency, intensity, and duration of extreme heat events in many regions, consistent with a warming climate. Public health research separately and consistently links extreme heat exposure to increased mortality and morbidity, particularly among the vulnerable groups named above, and to compounding effects with air quality (heat often coincides with higher ground-level ozone, connecting back to the air pollution risks discussed elsewhere on this blog).

## Where my opinion comes in

The evidence establishes that heat is dangerous and likely to become more frequent. What I'll add as personal opinion is this: I think public health systems and public communication chronically under-invest in heat as an emergency category, relative to its actual toll, because it doesn't fit the visual and narrative pattern we associate with disasters. A named storm gets warnings, evacuation orders, and rolling news coverage. A heatwave, even a dangerous one, often gets a passing mention in the weather segment. I think that mismatch costs lives, particularly among people who don't have the resources to self-protect — reliable air conditioning, flexible work schedules, or someone checking in on them.

> A hazard doesn't have to look like a disaster to act like one. In my view, extreme heat is the clearest current example of that gap between appearance and actual risk.

What would a better response look like, in my opinion? Early-warning systems tied to concrete actions (cooling centres, outreach to at-risk residents), building codes and urban design that reduce indoor heat exposure, workplace heat-safety standards for outdoor labour, and — frankly — media coverage that treats a severe heatwave with something closer to the urgency given to other extreme weather events. None of this is a radical claim; it's largely already recommended by public health and emergency management researchers. The gap, as I see it, is implementation and attention, not knowledge.

### Key takeaways

- **Evidence:** Extreme heat events are becoming more frequent and intense in many regions, and are consistently linked to increased mortality, especially among vulnerable groups.
- **Evidence:** Heat-related deaths are frequently under-recorded because heat often contributes to cardiovascular or respiratory death rather than being logged as the primary cause.
- **Opinion:** The author believes heat receives disproportionately little emergency-level public attention relative to its actual health toll, compared with more visually dramatic hazards.
- **Opinion:** The author argues implementation and public attention, not scientific knowledge, are the main gaps in an effective heat response.
